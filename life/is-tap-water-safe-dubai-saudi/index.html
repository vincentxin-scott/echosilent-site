Is Tap Water Safe to Drink in Dubai and Saudi Arabia?

A straightforward answer on whether tap water is safe to drink in the UAE and Saudi Arabia, plus why many people still filter it.

S
SilentEcho Editors
· July 31, 2026

If you are moving to Dubai, Riyadh, or Jeddah, you will probably stop drinking tap water within your first week. Not because it is dangerous, but because it tastes different and the locals do not drink it. The question is whether that habit is based on fact or just on taste and culture.

The short answer: tap water in the UAE and Saudi Arabia is legally safe to drink. It meets local and international standards for potable water. The longer answer: safe does not always mean pleasant, and many residents choose to filter, boil, or buy bottled water for taste, comfort, and peace of mind.

Where tap water comes from in the UAE

The UAE relies heavily on desalinated seawater. Dubai Electricity and Water Authority (DEWA) and its counterparts in Abu Dhabi, Sharjah, and other emirates run large-scale desalination plants. The water is treated, chlorinated, and tested against strict standards before it enters the distribution network.

In theory, the water coming out of the desalination plant is clean. The risk usually comes from the last mile: old building pipes, rooftop storage tanks, and internal plumbing. If the building is older or the tanks are not cleaned regularly, water quality can decline between the plant and your tap.

Where tap water comes from in Saudi Arabia

Saudi Arabia uses a mix of desalinated seawater and non-renewable groundwater. The desalinated water supply is treated to potable standards. In rural areas and older cities, groundwater may contain higher levels of minerals, including fluoride and salinity.

As in the UAE, the weakest link is usually the building's internal plumbing and storage tanks, not the source water.

What the authorities say

  • DEWA (Dubai): tap water meets World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines and UAE standards. DEWA publishes regular water quality reports.
  • Abu Dhabi Distribution Company (ADDC): similarly states that tap water is safe for drinking.
  • Saudi National Water Company: desalinated water supplied to households meets national drinking water standards.

These are official positions. They do not mean every tap in every building produces perfect water, but they do mean the water is treated and monitored at the source.

Why most residents still do not drink it

Even when tap water is officially safe, residents in the Gulf overwhelmingly avoid drinking it straight. There are a few reasons:

1. Taste

Desalinated water has a different mineral profile from natural spring water. It often tastes "flat" or slightly metallic. The chlorine used for disinfection can also leave a smell or taste that people dislike.

2. Building-level contamination

Water sits in rooftop storage tanks and travels through building pipes before reaching your tap. If those tanks are not cleaned regularly, sediment, bacteria, and algae can build up. This is a real issue in some older buildings.

3. Habit and culture

Bottled water is cheap and widely available. Most Gulf residents grew up drinking bottled water, and the habit carries over. It is a social norm, not just a health decision.

4. Trust gap

Even when official reports say the water is safe, many people trust a sealed bottle more than water that has travelled through pipes they cannot see.

Is it actually safe to drink?

For most people in most buildings, yes — especially for short-term visitors. If you are staying in a modern hotel or a new apartment building, the water is almost certainly fine to drink from a health perspective.

If you are in an older building, if the tap water has a strong chlorine smell, or if you have a sensitive stomach, filtering or boiling is a reasonable precaution. Vulnerable groups — infants, elderly people, pregnant women, and people with compromised immune systems — may also prefer filtered or bottled water.

What about bottled water vs filtered water?

Bottled water is the default choice for most Gulf residents. It is convenient, tastes consistent, and feels safer. But it has downsides:

  • Cost: a family of four in Dubai typically spends AED 200-400 per month on bottled water. Over a year, that is AED 2,400-4,800.
  • Plastic waste: even if bottles are recycled, the environmental cost is significant.
  • Storage: bottles take up space in small apartments and villas.
  • Quality variation: not all bottled water brands are equal. Some local brands have mineral profiles that taste very different from international brands.

Filtered tap water is the cheaper and more sustainable option long-term. A mid-tier filter system costs AED 1,200-1,800 upfront and AED 600-1,000 per year in filters. Compared to AED 2,400-4,800 per year for bottled water, the payback period is 6-12 months.

For taste-sensitive households, a Brita pitcher or under-sink carbon filter removes chlorine taste and sediment. For households concerned about dissolved solids or old building plumbing, a reverse osmosis system provides water close to bottled quality.

How to test your own tap water at home

If you are unsure about your building's water quality, you can do two simple checks:

  1. TDS meter: a handheld meter costs AED 30-50. It measures total dissolved solids — not contamination, but a useful indicator of mineral content. Dubai tap water typically reads 100-250 ppm. RO water reads 10-50 ppm. Bottled water varies from 50-200 ppm depending on the brand.
  2. Chlorine test strips: check if your water has a strong chlorine residual. Some chlorine is necessary for safety; too much affects taste.

If your TDS reading is above 300 ppm, or if the water has a strong smell, discolouration, or visible particles, contact your building management and consider filtering more aggressively.

You do not need to choose between raw tap water and expensive bottled water. A good water filter removes chlorine taste, sediment, and most bacteria while keeping the cost far below buying bottles every week. For a full comparison, see our guide to the best water filters for Gulf homes.

A simple countertop pitcher like the Brita Style XL improves taste for under AED 150. A reverse osmosis system like the AquaTru Classic or an under-sink unit like the Pureit Vital Max can bring the water closer to bottled quality.

For a typical family of four in Dubai, buying bottled water costs AED 200-400 per month. A filter system pays for itself in 2-3 months and produces far less plastic waste.

The bottom line

Tap water in Dubai and Saudi Arabia is legally safe to drink. It is treated, tested, and monitored. However, taste, building-level plumbing, and local habit mean most residents filter it or buy bottled water.

If you are in a new building and do not mind the taste, tap water is fine. If you want better taste, less scale in your appliances, and more confidence in your building's tanks, a water filter is the practical middle ground.
